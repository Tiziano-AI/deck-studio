# Composition and visual judgment

Use this to turn an argument into slide surfaces or diagnose a deck that reads
poorly despite technically correct layout. Template choice is only one input.

## Give each slide a job and a dominant relationship

The slide's job may be to establish a fact, explain a mechanism, compare viable
options, show change, orient a section, or invite an exercise. Choose the visual
form from that relationship: a timeline for sequence, aligned columns for
comparable alternatives, an annotated chart for quantitative evidence, a diagram
for a mechanism, or a large image when a specific thing must be seen.

Make the intended first read evident through scale, position and contrast. The
headline gives the point, the dominant visual or evidence carries it, and
supporting text resolves ambiguity. Equal-sized boxes imply equal importance;
arbitrary card grids can erase the argument. Proximity implies grouping and
arrows imply a relationship, often a cause or flow. Do not draw those claims
unless the source supports them.

## Density depends on use

A speaker-led slide can reveal one idea at a time and leave explanation to
speech. A reading slide needs the claim and its evidence together, and the
implication when the reader would not draw it.
High density can be excellent if the hierarchy and reading order stay clear;
sparse slides can fail a costly decision when the evidence is missing.

Use a limited typographic hierarchy, stable alignment and consistent spacing
to help a reader distinguish argument from detail. Give tables and charts enough
room for labels at the intended viewing size. If content does not fit, first
remove redundancy, redistribute emphasis or split a compound slide without
losing its sequence. Shrinking everything disguises a structural problem.
Removing a qualification to fit is a semantic change and must update the source
within the user's authority. Stating a qualification once, where it applies, is
not removing it.

Establish the hardest content-bearing layout early. A beautiful title page
cannot establish whether the system supports a pricing table, architecture
diagram or three-option decision. Adapt or reject the system when it fails the
material; do not turn the material into decorative filler to save the template.

## Visual register and rhythm

State the visual intent concretely: institutional dossier, editorial argument,
field research notebook, technical explanation or product cinema, for example.
Tie it to the actual audience and topic. "Modern" by itself is not a design
decision. Restrained typography and high information density can carry authority;
a cinematic crop and generous silence can make a reveal memorable. Neither is
universally better.

Let rhythm follow the argument: a section change may need a pause; a comparison
needs stable structure; a sequence may reuse a layout so the changing element
is unmistakable. Repetition is useful when it teaches how to read the deck.
Variation earns its place when the slide's job changes. Decorative gradients,
badges, blobs and cards should not replace hierarchy, data semantics or brand.

Color must work beyond hue alone. Pair risk or category colors with labels,
symbols or position. Use motion to stage comprehension, not to conceal the
static page required by PDF. Check the captured state when a meaningful reveal
or animation is present.

## Inspect the actual experience

Read the deck in order, including headline transitions, to test whether the
argument survives without your working context. Inspect every slide at the
intended size, not only a contact-sheet thumbnail. Check navigation, dense
evidence, crops, source labels, font loading and consistent visual meaning.
Phone letterbox screenshots establish scaling, not mobile-native readability.

Reject accidental clipping, overlap, unreadable labels, generic chrome without
a purpose, internal process language in the audience's frame and changed claims
disguised as visual polish. A source comparison is necessary because a beautiful
render can still omit the user's qualification or reverse a comparison.

Record the actual visual rationale and remaining trade-off in the acceptance
record. Do not manufacture a defect to fill the weakness field: an intended
trade-off such as reading density versus stage impact is useful when real.
The user can choose taste; the producing agent remains responsible for obvious
usability and fit defects. [Visual acceptance](visual-acceptance.md) owns the
byte-bound record, while [rendered QA](rendered-qa.md) explains mechanical
signals that inform this judgment.
