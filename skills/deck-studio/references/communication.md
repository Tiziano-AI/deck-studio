# Communication: audience, argument and evidence

Use this when the message is unresolved or a revision changes what the audience
should understand. For the complete archetypes, length ranges, density choices
and characteristic mistakes, consult [DECKS.md](../DECKS.md). Existing approved
content does not need another intake pass.

## The audience's task determines the argument

Begin with what a particular receiver must understand, decide, believe or do.
The requested filename alone does not settle this. "Pitch" may mean a cold
investor introduction, a customer decision, a partnership or an internal budget
request; each has different skepticism, evidence and next steps.

A live presentation can use voice, pause and progressive disclosure. An async
deck must carry, without narration, the context, logic, qualifications and
evidence its reader does not already have. A forwarded leave-behind also needs
to survive the loss of the initial meeting's shared context; a deck for the
people who were in it does not restate that context. Adding paragraphs to a live
deck rarely supplies that structure.

What the reader already knows, which of the material they wrote or decided, and
how they relate to the sender also set the register, before any slide is
written. [DECKS.md](../DECKS.md) defines the explanatory register for a reader
who lacks the context and the peer register for one who shares it, wrote the
material or knows the field better than the writer. They are the audience
registers Adaptive Communication names, where that guidance is loaded; its
working register serves the backstage source and the account to the requester,
not slide copy.

Resolve consequential unknowns from supplied materials first. If necessary ask
the question that changes the work, rather than collecting every field:

| Uncertainty | Useful question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audience/outcome | Who must do what after seeing this? | Thesis, ask and scope. |
| Mode | Will it be presented or read without you? | Density and self-contained explanation. |
| Reader knowledge | What do they already know, and which of this material did they write or decide? | Register ([DECKS.md](../DECKS.md)) and what stays unexplained. |
| Skepticism | What alternative or objection will they compare this against? | Evidence and countercase. |
| Proof | What do we actually know, and from where? | Claim strength and missing work. |
| Destination | Must colleagues edit it or use assistive reading/search? | Artifact owner before rendering. |

For overloaded notes, read them before asking what they contain. Separate
decision-relevant evidence, supporting detail and background. For an executive
decision, find viable options and the recommendation, not only a topic. For a
workshop, identify what participants will produce or learn to do and how the
session elicits it. A deadline can justify an explicit assumption; it cannot
turn absent evidence into a fact.

## Build the argument, then its sequence

A narrative spine joins the audience's starting understanding to the desired
one. It has a thesis, evidence in an intelligible order, the trade-off or
objection that matters and a next action. Make these relationships explicit in
the semantic source; the sequence should not be a list of unrelated topics.

An executive decision often starts with the recommendation, then options,
trade-offs and evidence. A persuasive explanation may need context, tension,
solution, proof and implications before an ask. Fundraising can need company
purpose, problem, solution, why now, market, alternatives, economics, traction,
team and ask; the company's stage decides which belong. None is a universal
slide order. Teaching may instead need example, concept, application, practice
and feedback.

Give a slide a claim or useful question rather than only a category label.
"Margin pressure is concentrated in two accounts" tells a reader how to use
the chart; "Financials" does not. Section dividers, exercises and intentional
reveals can have other jobs; in the peer register, a plain label beats a
headline that counts or restates a body that is already the point
([DECKS.md](../DECKS.md)). Keep necessary reasoning in the main flow; an
appendix should deepen a sound argument, not contain the premise needed to
understand it.

## Evidence fits the claim and the receiver

For each material claim, distinguish the observation, source assertion,
interpretation, estimate and recommendation. Keep the date, population,
baseline, unit and scope that can change its meaning. An example demonstrates
possibility, not prevalence; a benchmark with incomparable populations does not
establish superiority. A future target is not traction.

Decide what would satisfy this audience's real skepticism. An enterprise buyer
may need implementation risk and reference evidence; an investor may need growth
economics; a board may need variance, causes and a decision. More citations do
not substitute for the evidence they need. Verify unstable external facts at
their relevant source before treating them as current. Keep unavailable proof
visible in the working source and calibrate the claim, rather than inventing
data to complete a template.

Source labels and audience-relevant limitations belong near the evidence they
qualify. Internal validator status, unsupported-claim notes and publication
permission do not belong in slide chrome. Rewrite a real limitation naturally,
for example "Pilot results cover one site over six weeks", without removing it.

## One source through revision

Use an existing brief, approved outline or deck as the meaning owner. When none
exists, a small Markdown outline can carry audience, intended action, mode,
thesis and ordered slides with claims/evidence/qualifications. The optional
[brief template](../assets/deck-discovery-brief.md) helps complex cross-artifact
jobs; it is not an obligatory deliverable for a simple deck.

Distinguish a source the agent has settled for production from meaning the user
explicitly locked. Reuse either through visual execution. The agent can reopen
its own choices within the mandate, explicitly updating the source before
propagating the change. Ask before altering a user lock or a consequential
choice not delegated. Style-only variants retain the same meaning; alternative
messages are labeled narrative exploration. New evidence may justify revision,
but should not silently change what an approved deck says.
